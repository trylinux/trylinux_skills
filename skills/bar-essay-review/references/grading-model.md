# How NCBE written grading works (background)

Use this to explain scores to the user. It does not change the grading procedure.

- **Who grades.** Each jurisdiction grades the MEE and MPT. NCBE writes the grading materials (MEE analyses and MPT drafters' point sheets) and trains graders at a workshop held right after each exam.
- **Workshop.** For each item, graders silently read real examinee answers, grade them, and discuss outliers. Grading materials can be refined or reweighted afterward.
- **Relative grading.** NCBE recommends rank-ordering: the best papers in a grader's pile get the top score even if imperfect, and the weakest get the bottom score even if they contain some content. NCBE uses a 1-6 scale because it is memorable and avoids bunching in the middle. Absolute grading is acceptable only if it is consistent and still spreads scores.
- **Calibration.** Graders read and grade common sets of about 10 papers, compare, and resolve differences, repeating until they agree. They keep benchmark papers for each score and track how many papers land at each score to make sure they use the whole scale.
- **Bunching wastes an item.** If most papers get the same score, the item contributes little to separating examinees.
- **Scaling.** Raw written scores are scaled to the jurisdiction's MBE mean and standard deviation. Scaling preserves rank order, so what matters is how a paper compares with other papers on the same exam, not an absolute percentage. This also corrects for harder or easier questions and stronger or weaker cohorts (February cohorts typically score lower than July).
- **Weights on the UBE.** MBE 50%, MEE 30%, MPT 20%.
- **Writing.** Written communication is part of what the MEE and MPT measure. Good writing means organized, explains the law, applies it to the facts, and explains the reasoning. It does not mean legalese.
- **Development.** Items and grading materials go through roughly two to three years of drafting, outside expert review, and pretesting with newly licensed lawyers before use.
