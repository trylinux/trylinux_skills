# Grading the MEE

Read this before grading a Multistate Essay Examination answer.

## Format
- Six 30-minute essays per administration. Each question has a fact pattern and one or more numbered calls.
- Starting July 2026, Conflict of Laws, Family Law, Trusts and Estates, and Secured Transactions are no longer tested on the MEE. Family Law and Trusts and Estates move to the MPT through February 2028. An older practice question may still test a dropped subject; grade it normally, but mention that the subject is no longer tested on the MEE if that matters to the user's study plan.

## Anatomy of an NCBE MEE analysis
Knowing the structure tells you how to turn it into a rubric.

1. **Subject tag** at the top, with a subject-matter outline code.
2. **Disclaimer:** the analysis is illustrative of excellent answers, more detailed than examinees are expected to write, and not a model answer. It may be out of date if the law has changed.
3. **Legal Problems:** a numbered list of the questions the drafters intended to test. This is the issue list.
4. **Summary:** the conclusions on each problem in a paragraph or two.
5. **Points:** each Point begins with its conclusion in the first sentence, then states the rule with authority, applies it to the facts, and concludes. Each Point carries a percentage weight; weights sum to 100%.
   - Weights are uneven. A single Point can range from about 15% to 40%. Missing a heavy Point is far more costly than missing a light one.
   - A Point can carry sub-weights (for example, one Point split between two distinct sub-issues). Score the sub-parts separately.
   - Each Point's discussion covers more than an excellent answer must. Use it to identify the issue, the rule, the key facts, and the conclusion. Do not treat each sentence as a required element (see SKILL.md Step 2).
   - A single call can map to more than one Point, and one Point can resolve more than one sub-question.
6. **Bracketed NOTES** inside or after a Point. These are direct instructions to graders. Treat them as binding.

## How to read the NOTES
NOTES fall into recurring types. Convert each into a rubric instruction:

| NOTE type | What it means for scoring |
|---|---|
| Doctrine X "is not relevant" / "no credit" | Zero credit for discussing it. Mark it under Wasted effort. |
| Discussion is accurate but "not responsive to the call" | Zero credit even though correct. |
| "The call does not ask about" X / examinee "need not discuss" X | Not required. No deduction for omitting it; no credit for including it. |
| The facts foreclose an issue (for example, a fact establishing a writing, or a stipulated majority rule) | Analysis of that issue is unnecessary. Do not reward it; do not penalize a one-line acknowledgment. |
| State variations are irrelevant because the facts specify the rule | Grade against the rule the facts give, not the examinee's preferred rule. |
| Older law or case-law route reaching the correct result "should receive substantial credit" | Full Issue and Conclusion, Rule at 0.10, Application as earned (about 0.75 for a sound answer). |
| Jurisdictions split; "no single analysis is correct" | Credit any well-reasoned answer that identifies the governing standard and applies it. Reward recognizing the split. |
| Two doctrinal framings reach the same result (for example, defense vs. element) | Either framing gets full credit if the outcome and reasoning are right. |
| Facts that are true but irrelevant to the question asked | A red herring. Using them to decide the wrong question costs credit on that Point. |
| Supplementary elements the analysis says are "present here" | Brief acknowledgment is enough for full credit on that sub-issue; extended discussion adds nothing. |

## What earns full credit on a Point
- Spots the issue the Point covers.
- States a correct rule. Paraphrase is fine. Case names and section numbers are not required.
- Applies the rule to the specific facts that matter, including facts that cut the other way.
- Reaches a conclusion that answers the call.

## What loses credit
- Conclusion without reasoning ("this plea is valid").
- Correct rule with no facts applied.
- Wrong rule leading to the right answer: Rule 0.10 at most (see SKILL.md Step 2).
- Wrong conclusion on a question with a clear answer: Conclusion 0, and Application at most 0.10, or 0.30 if the analysis was sound up to one misstep at the end. NCBE values reasoning over conclusions, but on a clear-answer question the wrong result usually means the reasoning failed.
- Assuming facts not in the question to reach a result: Application capped at 0.10 on that Point. NCBE tells examinees not to assume facts.
- Applying a specific state's law when the question does not call for it: grade against generally accepted principles (the UBE instruction). No penalty if the answer reaches the generally accepted result; lost credit if the local rule leads it elsewhere.
- Answering a question the call did not ask (for example, discussing a remedy when only liability was asked).

## Governing law on the UBE
UBE examinees are instructed to answer under generally accepted fundamental legal principles rather than local case or statutory law, unless the question itself specifies a rule (for example, "State A follows the traditional doctrine"). Do not grade a UBE practice answer against Kentucky or any other state's law.

## Relief and remedy questions
Many MEE calls turn on what relief a court can actually grant, not just who wins. Check whether the answer states the correct remedy or procedural outcome, not just the merits.

## Length expectations
An answer written in 30 minutes is much shorter than the analysis. Do not compare word counts. Compare issue coverage and the quality of application. If the question is not an MEE, find out the time allowed (ask, or infer from the exam: for example, Georgia gives four essays in a three-hour session, about 45 minutes each) and scale expectations accordingly.

## State-drafted essays (non-UBE jurisdictions)
Some users will submit essays from a state that writes its own questions (Georgia, Florida, Pennsylvania, and others). These differ from the MEE:
- **Governing law.** The instructions name a state. Grade under that state's law where the examinee states a state rule and it is consistent with what you know. Where you cannot verify a state-specific rule, say so in the Note column and give credit for a correct generally accepted rule unless you know the state departs from it. Do not deduct for a UCC or Restatement rule that the state also follows.
- **Call structure.** Expect more calls (three or four) with sub-parts, and calls that ask for advocacy ("argue for X"), numbers ("estimate the damages"), or strategy ("what should the client do"). Build one rubric row per call or sub-part, weighted by the call's emphasis; do not force the MEE "Legal Problems" structure.
- **Advocacy calls.** Score the strength of the argument: does it lead with the best available theory, marshal the supporting facts, and answer the obvious counter? A weaker but legitimate theory caps Application at 0.30. Do not score a "conclusion"; score Conclusion on whether the argument lands where its side needs it to.
- **Numeric calls.** The measure of recovery is the point; arithmetic is secondary. Correct measure with a slip: full Rule, Application 0.30. Wrong measure (for example, cost instead of contract price): Rule 0, even if the arithmetic is clean. Failing to say that alternative remedies (expectation vs. reliance) do not stack: Application 0.30 at most on that row.
- **Rubric ceiling.** State-published sample answers that received high scores routinely contain a doctrinal error or a thin section. Build the rubric to what a strong answer does in the time allowed, and use the drafted-rubric band table in SKILL.md Step 3.
- **No official analysis.** Most of these states publish sample high-scoring answers, not analyses. If the user supplies one, use it as a benchmark for the top of the pool (band 5-6), not as a rubric; its errors tell you what real graders forgave.
